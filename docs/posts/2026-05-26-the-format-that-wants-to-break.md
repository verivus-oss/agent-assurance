# The Format That Wants To Break: A Clean-Room Reading of DAG-TOML

Most file formats spend their effort being forgiving. They try to keep parsing when a field is missing, to keep signatures valid across small edits, to keep readers compatible across versions, to keep the world working even when the world has drifted. The DAG-TOML specification (the public artefact this repository defines) does the opposite, on purpose, in several places at once. It is a format that wants to break when the things it describes have changed, and it goes to some trouble to make the breakage loud and structural rather than quiet and recoverable.

Reading the spec cold, that is the first thing that stands out. The second is that the brittleness is not an accident of inexperience; it is the central design move, defended on every page where it shows up. To make sense of that move, the question to start with is not "what does this spec say?" but "what problem is this spec an answer to?", because the answer is unusual, and most of the awkward-looking choices fall out of it cleanly.

## The problem this spec exists to solve

The framing the project has been developing in a companion essay set (kept in a separate working repository, not part of this public spec repo) is sharper than the framing the spec adopts in its own introduction, and the two illuminate each other. The essays open with what is, in the end, an epistemology argument (developed at length in the in-house essay "Free Is Never Free: Why Zero Trust Must Move Up the Abstraction Stack", drawing on Daniel Kahneman, Annie Duke, Kathryn Schulz, and Steven Levitt). Kahneman shows that humans confuse coherence with truth: System 1 hands us a fast answer, and System 2 frequently arrives later as counsel for the defence rather than as auditor. Annie Duke separates decision quality from outcome quality (a good outcome does not prove a good process), and the analogue in software is that *process validity is not semantic validity*. Kathryn Schulz adds the human texture: being wrong does not feel like being wrong; it feels like being right until reality interrupts. The Freakonomics-shaped point lands the argument: we have to stop asking only whether a story coheres and start asking what would actually prove the thing is still what we thought it was.

The 2026 npm Shai-Hulud / TanStack incident is the load-bearing example because it forces the distinction into the open. As reported by StepSecurity, Snyk, and Cloud Security Alliance (and summarised in the source essay cited above), malicious packages were published with valid SLSA Build Level 3 provenance, by the project's own legitimate release pipeline, after attacker-controlled code hijacked the runner mid-workflow through OIDC token theft. The provenance was not fake. It proved exactly the thing it was designed to prove: where the package was built, and from which source commit. It did not prove that the build environment had remained semantically intact between commit and publication. The signature was right; the *meaning* was compromised.

If you accept that framing (and the spec asks you to), then the entire conventional supply-chain stack has been solving a real problem that turns out to be only half of the actual problem. SLSA, in-toto, Sigstore are answers to "who signed this, and where did it come from." The question that hits an autonomous-infrastructure context harder is "did the thing remain within its declared class, scope, and meaning as it moved downstream?" That question has a name in the project's framing, **Provable Intent** plus **Structural Governance**, and it is the question DAG-TOML is built around.

The picture sharpens further when you put two threats next to each other:

The first is the **counterfeit matryoshka doll** (developed in the in-house essay "The Counterfeit Matryoshka Doll Problem"): a trusted inner component, wrapped in a malicious outer layer, whose signature is valid because the outer layer was signed correctly. The attacker did not break the trusted content; they composed around it and hijacked the execution path. Reading checks the outer signature; the outer signature is fine; the audit passes; the wrapper has just smuggled the equivalent of a hex digit into a decimal field. The class shifted. The downstream consumer was not told.

The second is **semantic laundering** (the threat described in the in-house essay "AI Launders Code: But We Can Make Human Insight Un-losable"): an AI agent rewrites a piece of logic so thoroughly that the surface text no longer matches anything anyone has on file, but the underlying call graph and dependency shape is unchanged. Traditional detectors read code like a novel; if the words change, they conclude the story has changed. The structure tells a different story. Surface change is now nearly free; structural change is the thing that actually means anything. DAG-TOML does not perform the structural check itself (that is indexer work, the job of tools in the `sqry` family). It is the substrate where the *results* of those checks must be cryptographically bound, and the format where an agent's declared intent gets pinned against the structure of what was actually produced.

Both threats share a property. They both *defeat checks aimed at the surface* by leaving the surface plausible. The defence against both is the same: stop trusting the surface, and start binding identity and capability to the structural shape underneath. The format does its half of that defence and refuses to do the rest, which is most of the point.

This is the problem DAG-TOML is an answer to. It is agent workflow planning, but that is not the interesting part; the interesting part is that the planning artefact becomes a typed, brittle evidence surface that breaks visibly when its upstream world shifts, and ties an agent's declared intent to the descriptor that authorised it. The format itself is small enough to read in one sitting and uncomfortable enough to use that the discomfort is the load-bearing part.

## The design moves that follow

Once the problem is named that way, the unusual-looking parts of the spec stop being unusual.

**Format, not runtime.** Worth saying first, because most of the other moves are easier to read once it is in mind. The spec is fierce about what it does not do. It is a format. It does not run agents, schedule work, sign envelopes, manage keys, transport readiness signals, enforce embargoes, or compute behavioural call-graph subsets. Those are runtime concerns and the spec refuses to absorb them (the test SPEC §1 and architecture.md §3 offer is the simplest one: "if I changed this, would conforming files have to change?"). The discipline pays off twice. The spec stays small enough to read in one sitting, and runtimes built against it stay plural rather than collapsing into a single reference implementation that becomes the de facto spec. The conformance contract lives in the descriptors and the validators; the executable surface is intentionally minimal.

**The closure root is the brittleness-propagation rule (for spec-reserved kinds).** Every conforming document of a **spec-reserved** `template_kind` (one declared in `core/*-kind.toml`, in a spec-reserved profile's descriptor set, or the two meta kinds `kind-descriptor` and `ontology`) carries a `closure_root` field, a SHA-256 (or stronger) digest taken over an ordered concatenation of every upstream artefact hash the document cites plus every upstream revocation snapshot known at emit time. The narrower scoping matters: non-spec-reserved template kinds (process artefacts such as review bundles, claim-analysis finding sets, internal scratch files) are out of conformance scope for §12, and producers who want a file *outside* the brittleness graph give it a non-spec-reserved kind on purpose. When any upstream input changes (its bytes, its revocation status, or, crucially, the kind descriptor it cites), the downstream `closure_root` recomputes to a different value, and any signed envelope wrapping the downstream document is now structurally invalid until a fresh signing ceremony. SPEC §12.8 is candid that the byte-level canonicalisation algorithm (length-prefixing, delimiter handling, collation, duplicate handling) is deliberately deferred for v1.0.0; what is normative now is the cascade-break property, not a single canonical concatenation. This is the inversion of what conventional PKI provides. X.509, CMS, PGP and friends preserve signature validity when *upstream artefacts* change, with revocation handled out-of-band, so a downstream consumer may continue to verify a signature even when the world the signature implicitly depended on has shifted. The closure-root rule makes that impossible by design. The point is that a valid signature on an artefact whose upstream world has shifted is a *misleading* signature, and the spec would rather force a fresh ceremony than let a stale one persist. The articles call this Zero Trust at the layer of meaning. The spec calls it brittleness as a feature, not a bug. Both phrases are exact.

**Abstraction class and capability envelope are the answer to the matryoshka problem.** Closure-root catches identity drift; on its own it does not catch the case where bytes are stable and the class is wrong (the 2024 compression-library backdoor SPEC §13 cites is the named example, although the cleanest SLSA-passing-with-compromised-meaning incident in the public record is the TanStack one mentioned above). The spec's declaration surface is to let each kind descriptor carry an `abstraction_class` (a versioned identifier like `data-transform.v1`, in the producer's own taxonomy) and a `capability_envelope` (a closed set of capability domains drawn from WASI Preview 2: `filesystem`, `sockets`, `http`, `clocks`, `random`, `environment`, `process_spawn`, `ipc`, `crypto_keys`, plus separate `cpu_bounds` and `memory_bounds` for resource limits). A domain whose grant table is missing is read as denied; the default is fail-closed. The class declaration is part of the descriptor's canonical bytes and flows into its closure root, so widening an envelope cascade-breaks every downstream instance that cited the old class. The format does the declaration and the cascade-break. The *attenuation calculus* (the rule that a child envelope must be a subset of its parent envelope, the principle of *intent-diminution* the matryoshka article calls for) is referenced by SPEC §13.5 but pinned in a separate executable specification, not in DAG-TOML itself. Likewise the enforcement backend (the runtime that decides whether observed behaviour actually fits inside the declared envelope) is runtime-owned. The format gives consumers something specific to point at and refuse against; it does not prove the runtime behaved.

**The IJB substrate is the radical-decomposition floor.** Underneath the kind descriptors and ontology files sits a deliberately small typology of six primitives (`thing`, `scope`, `path`, `observed`, `constraint`, `time`), and every declaration in the ontology and every block in a kind descriptor carries an annotation pinning it to one of those six. The articles describe the move at the conceptual level: radical decomposition to the smallest meaningful units, followed by bounded reconstruction where each layer maintains strict logical consistency within its iteration. IJB is that move, made enforceable inside the typed surface. No contributor can sneak a new category into the ontology or a kind descriptor without the conformance validator noticing the missing or wrong primitive annotation. The validator does *not* parse prose; SPEC §10.3 acknowledges that the spec deliberately admits free-text outside IJB's strict canonical assertion grammar, and §10.4 leaves forbidden-concept matching out of v1.0.0 scope. The discipline is on the structured contract, not on every sentence in the document.

**TOML as both format and contract, with no JSON Schema sidecar.** The format is TOML, and the contract describing the format is also TOML, the `*-kind.toml` descriptor files (the meta kinds `kind-descriptor` and `ontology` carry their own descriptions; SPEC §2.4 stops the recursion deliberately, saying tooling must not require a `kind-descriptor-kind.toml` to exist). There is no parallel JSON Schema, and the spec is explicit about not wanting one. A JSON Schema would be a second declaration of what the descriptors already declare, would cover a strict subset (structure only, never graph-shaped invariants), and would create exactly the synchronisation hazard the descriptor pattern was introduced to eliminate. If editor tooling eventually demands JSON Schema, the spec's answer is to *generate* it from the descriptors at build time, not to hand-author a second source of truth. This is a small choice that prevents an entire family of long-term drift problems, and it reflects the broader posture: a format whose primitive moves are silent coercion (YAML's Norway problem is the canonical example) is exactly wrong for this context. The spec wants ambiguity to fail loudly.

**Producer-side responsibility, not consumer-side.** A consumer of a DAG-TOML document should verify only the last artefact. The producer carries the burden of making provenance possible for the next consumer in the chain. This is the inversion that makes the whole brittleness graph work. The cascade-break property only has teeth if every producer in the chain emits again with a refreshed closure when their upstream changes; consumers who tried to do their own upstream traversal would defeat the locality of the verification model. The spec's design says: do less, but do it in the right place. The right place is upstream of the people relying on you.

**Separation of duty (INV06).** The Agent Assurance Profile contains an invariant in its `gate-decision-kind.toml` descriptor that, in a particular case, carries enormous weight: when a gate-decision's `decision.subject_class = "self-modification"`, the `deciding_provider_id` and `deciding_model_family_id` MUST both differ from the proposing agent's provider and model family. The articles' "multi-party attestation for material changes" rendered as a structural rule, scoped precisely to the case where it matters most. Ordinary gates (the cases where `subject_class` is anything else) may still be single-provider, or even single-signer depending on the tier the operation has adopted. What the profile rules out, structurally, is the cheapest and most tempting failure mode of agentic systems: the model that closes its own self-modification ticket. The agent-assurance profile is therefore positioned for multi-provider environments specifically because the strongest assurance rule cannot be satisfied without them. Single-provider deployments can still use ordinary gates and core DAG-TOML; what they cannot achieve is the full self-modification assurance the profile reserves for that case.

Taken together, every load-bearing rule sits at a place where the format refuses to be flexible. The format does its half of the work and is candid about the half it leaves to runtimes. Adopters who file the brittlenesses off in pursuit of ergonomics end up with a format that does not solve the problem the spec is an answer to.

## What the format does not prove

The clearest way to keep the spec from being oversold is to be plain about what it does *not* deliver, because the conventional supply-chain narrative would have a reader expect it to deliver all of these.

It does not perform signing ceremonies or manage keys. The closure root is part of a document's signable content; the spec is silent on which signing infrastructure wraps it. Existing PKI, eIDAS QES with declared intent, CAdES commitment-type-indication, FIDO2 with user-presence, all of those are legitimate envelopes around a DAG-TOML document, and the format pins none of them.

It does not enforce capability envelopes against runtime behaviour. The descriptor declares the envelope and the cascade-break property locks in any change to it. Whether a process actually stays inside that envelope is a job for a WASI-shaped runtime, an admission controller, or a separate behavioural audit. The spec gives consumers a contract surface to point at, and that is the whole of what the spec promises here.

It does not pin a byte-level canonical-concatenation algorithm. SPEC §12.8 is explicit that v1.0.0 normatively defines the cascade-break property and the closure inputs, and defers length-prefixing, sort key, and delimiter choices to a future pin so that cross-implementation closure-root agreement remains stable across the implementations expected to ship.

It does not run agents, schedule work, retry failures, or pick where state lives. architecture.md is firm: those belong to per-repository runtimes, control planes, and consumer tooling. The format is one of four roles in that picture; it is the one that does not move.

It does not enforce embargoes against wall-clock time, verify selective-disclosure proofs cryptographically, run forbidden-concept matchers across prose, or compute structural call-graph subset relations. Each of those is named, in the spec or in supporting articles, as a job for someone else.

This is the half of the bargain that adopters often hope to ignore. Ignoring it produces the failure mode the matryoshka article warns about: a format that declares classes that nobody enforces, signatures that nobody re-issues when their inputs change, gates that nobody routes through a second deciding provider. The format is a contract surface; it has the property of being right only when something else does the verification work the surface invites.

## What the format declares beyond core

Three spec-reserved profiles ship in the public repository besides core DAG-TOML. None of them are unusual extension points; they are first-class peers, each with its own profile descriptor and its own ontology, each independently selectable via the root `[meta].framework_profile`. Together they cover most of the surface a real operation needs and the article so far has glossed over.

The **Agent Assurance Profile** (`framework_profile = "agent-assurance"`, internal codename AGDF) adds the nine assurance kinds: `spec-contract`, `threat-model`, `smoke-validation`, `rollback-plan`, `adapter-contract`, `adapter-registry-binding`, `assertion-bundle`, `assertion-log-record`, `gate-decision`. The INV06 self-modification rule is the most visible piece; the rest of the profile is where the threat-model, the smoke validation, and the rollback plan get attached as named artefacts a runtime can read.

The **Disclosure Profile** (`framework_profile = "disclosure"`) adds three kinds for the case where a producer wants to publish a redacted view of a source artefact and still let a recipient verify the disclosure was faithful: `disclosure-attestation` (the signed posture statement), `redaction-manifest` (one row per redaction performed, with locator, method, and reason), and `selective-disclosure-proof` (the cryptographic commitment binding the manifest to the source). The proof's wire shape is RUNTIME-SPEC; the format names the proof family and records the bound source digest. A recipient combining the published bytes, the proof, and the manifest can confirm that the published bytes are a faithful redaction of the source without ever seeing the redacted bytes. This is the spec's answer for the audit-trail-with-PII case that an analytical essay on the closure-root rule otherwise has no good way of addressing.

The **Cost Profile** (`framework_profile = "cost"`) adds the `cost-record` kind for making agent token-costs, build-minute costs, and dependency-execution costs first-class, graph-bound artefacts. This is narrowly scoped on purpose. The spec is not interested in becoming a settlement clearinghouse or a micro-royalty market (the VAP article in the articles folder develops that vision, and the spec is correctly silent on it). The cost profile is the small, useful piece that earns its place: it lets a consumer ask "what did this artefact actually cost, end to end?" and get an answer that participates in the same brittleness graph as the rest of the evidence.

Three other parts of the public surface deserve naming because the rest of the article would otherwise leave them invisible. The `[provenance.encryption]` sub-table (SPEC §11.1) lets a producer declare that the source artefact was sealed (with a `hash_is_over = "ciphertext"` or `"plaintext"` shape) so a future consumer recomputing the source digest knows which bytes the digest committed to. The confidentiality vocabulary (SPEC §2.7) is closed (`public`, `restricted`, `confidential`, `trade-secret`, `embargoed`) and a file declaring `confidentiality = "embargoed"` is *structurally invalid* without an `embargo_until` companion field, which is one of the cleanest small examples of brittleness-as-feature in the entire spec. And the profile-descriptor kind (SPEC §6.1) is the machine-readable contract for a profile itself, recording its spec-reserved status, its contained kinds, its ontology pointer, its license, its confidentiality posture, and the profiles it extends; the namespacing partition (unprefixed names reserved for spec-reserved profiles, reverse-DNS names for private ones) is the entire infrastructure private adopters need.

## How it could be used

Read narrowly, DAG-TOML is just a file format, and the could-space is wide. A reasonable list, in roughly increasing ambition:

A single repository can use it as a planning substrate: an `implementation-dag` to coordinate a multi-step refactor, traceability entries as pieces land, a readiness-gate bundle as the PR template. None of this requires agents; it works as a discipline for human teams that want their planning artefacts to be machine-readable and their evidence claims to be auditable against the actual tree on disk. The brittleness machinery is mostly inert in this use, which is fine; the format does not refuse light use, it just does not justify its weight on light use alone.

A CI pipeline can lint planning bundles, refusing PRs whose evidence matrix lies about which tests cover which requirements, or whose DAG claims a `critical_path` that does not actually trace the longest weighted path through the dependency graph. The reference Python validators ship for this purpose. Safe-Rust and safe-Go primary validators exist for parts of the surface (profile-descriptor, disclosure, the meta surface, provenance encryption per README); other surfaces (closure-root, abstraction-class, rollback-trigger closure, IJB conformance) remain Python-only at v1.0.0 and will be ported as adoption requires.

A control plane can aggregate readiness across many repositories, pulling the review-readiness bundle from each and reducing it to a release-shaped go/no-go decision. The spec deliberately leaves transport and UI open; the wire format is the spec itself.

An organisation can adopt the Agent Assurance Profile to bind agent runtimes to a contract (gate-decision documents, adapter contracts, assertion bundles), and use the deployment-tier ladder (`solo ⊂ team ⊂ group ⊂ organisation ⊂ enterprise`) to scale assurance against the size of the operation. The tiers are not a schema feature; each tier file is a self-contained contract-declaration instance. The ladder is a documented adoption path, not a magic inheritance graph.

A regulated workflow (an audit trail for a healthcare AI, a compliance attestation for a financial system, a legal-grade record of who decided what when) can use the closure-root, abstraction-class, and disclosure-profile machinery to make falsification visibly hard. A tampered upstream artefact does not silently propagate; the cascade-break invalidates every signed envelope that depended on the old version, provided the producer has emitted the evidence the validator needs and a signing infrastructure outside the spec actually re-issues. An auditor told "nothing has changed upstream" can validate the declared closure inputs and check that the published evidence still adds up.

An ecosystem-scale tool (a package manager that wants to declare what abstraction class each dependency belongs to and refuse dependencies whose declared envelope does not match the consumer's expected one) can use the spec as the contract between publishers and consumers. The format declares the envelope and cascade-breaks on change. Proving that a dependency's runtime behaviour actually stays inside the declared envelope is a separate piece of infrastructure; the format makes the question askable in a uniform way.

The thing all of these have in common is that they treat the spec as a contract surface between actors who do not fully trust each other: between a human reviewer and an agent, between a repository and a release manager, between a publisher and a consumer, between an organisation and an auditor. The format does well exactly where that trust gap is the load-bearing part of the problem. It is over-built for cases where the gap does not exist, which is fine; the spec does not pretend to be the right tool for every job.

## How it should be used

Where it gets more interesting is in the places the design is doing something specific, and where adopters who do not understand the specificity will quickly file the rough edges off and lose the point. A clean-room reading turns up a small set of disciplines that the wrapping prose makes obvious but that are easy to drop under deadline pressure.

**Honour producer-side responsibility.** The single load-bearing piece of the design ethos is that responsibility lives with the producer. Adopters who push verification onto the consumer ("the reader can check upstream if they want to") have inverted the entire trust model. The brittleness property only works if every producer in the chain emits again with a refreshed closure when their upstream changes. Skip that step and the cascade-break degenerates into a polite suggestion.

**Treat brittleness as a feature, not a defect.** The most predictable adoption failure is the engineer who sees "every upstream change invalidates downstream signatures" and concludes the spec is broken and needs a backwards-compatibility layer. There are good reasons to be conservative about cascade-breaks in production (release windows, batched signing again, signed sentinel snapshots, the empty-closure sentinel for roots of the citation tree), and the spec accommodates all of them. But the brittleness itself is the point. A format that silently accepts upstream drift is a format that cannot tell you when your trust assumptions have stopped being true.

**Pair the closure root with the abstraction class, or accept you have only solved half the problem.** Closure-root alone catches *identity* drift. It does not catch the case the matryoshka attack actually is: bytes stable, class wrong. Adopters who deploy closure-roots without declaring abstraction classes and capability envelopes on their kind descriptors have hardened against half the problem and made no progress against the other half. The two are designed to be deployed together. Deploying only one leaves the gap any motivated adversary walks through.

**Take the separation-of-duty rule literally where it bites.** INV06 is structural for self-modification gate-decisions: the deciding provider and model family MUST differ from the proposing ones. Adopters running self-modifying agentic loops who route the gate-decision back to the same provider because it is faster, or because the multi-provider setup costs more, have not adopted the assurance profile; they have adopted a logo. For ordinary gates the rule is silent, which is correct; not every action needs cross-provider attestation. The discipline is to be honest about which actions count as self-modifying and route those to a different deciding provider. A profile description that quietly relaxes that scope has decided its format is something else.

**Generate, do not hand-author, derived schemas.** If editor tooling becomes important, derive the JSON Schema from the descriptors at build time and ship the result as a generated artefact. Do not hand-author a JSON Schema sidecar, however appealing it looks in a developer-experience review. Every hand-authored sidecar drifts; every drift is silent until it bites. The spec's no-JSON-Schema posture is a lesson learned from other formats, not an oversight.

**Pick a tier honestly.** The deployment-tier ladder is documented inheritance, but the inheritance is editorial, not structural; every tier file is a self-contained contract declaration. Pick the tier that matches your operation's actual capacity for review, attestation, and incident response, not the tier that sounds impressive. A `solo` tier honestly applied beats an `enterprise` tier full of empty fields.

**Use non-spec-reserved `template_kind` values for process artefacts that should not participate in the brittleness graph.** The closure-root rule binds to spec-reserved kinds. Process artefacts (review bundles, claim-analysis finding sets, internal scratch files) should use non-spec-reserved kinds, and adopters introducing private kinds should publish them under reverse-DNS profile names (`com.example.internal`, `org.acme.compliance`). The reverse-DNS partition is the entire namespacing infrastructure the spec relies on; the DNS namespace your organisation already owns provides uniqueness without a central registry.

**Use the disclosure and cost profiles where they fit.** An organisation that needs to publish partial evidence (say, an audit log where PII must be scrubbed) can use the disclosure profile to bind the redacted artefact cryptographically back to the source's closure root. The format does not allow hiding the fact that something was hidden. An organisation that runs many agents can use the cost profile to make per-action token costs first-class, graph-bound artefacts, so "what did this run actually cost?" has a closure-root-bound answer rather than a stack of opaque invoices.

**Do not invent runtime concerns and call them spec concerns.** Authentication, sandboxing, retry policy, transport, scheduler choice, persistence layout: these are runtime concerns by the spec's own test. The architecture document describes the boundary in detail; the right move is to honour it. Adopters who paper runtime concerns into custom kind descriptors will discover their internal extensions do not compose with anyone else's.

## What would make the spec hollow

The clearest way to understand a design is to consider the failure modes that would empty it of meaning, and the spec is unusually explicit about which ones it is willing to die for.

It would be hollow if the closure-root rule were softened for spec-reserved kinds, if upstream changes to a spec-reserved-kind closure did not have to cascade-break downstream signatures. The point would be lost on the first edit anyone made under deadline pressure. The spec acknowledges this by making the closure root a required field with a canonical empty-closure sentinel for documents that cite nothing upstream, so the brittleness graph remains a total function across spec-reserved kinds. There is no exception path for spec-reserved kinds. That is on purpose.

It would be hollow if the abstraction class were a hint rather than a contract, if a kind descriptor could declare a class and the runtime could ignore it. The cascade-break property only works if the class declaration is part of the descriptor's canonical bytes and flows into its closure root. The spec wires it in exactly that way, even though it deliberately leaves the runtime's enforcement of the envelope outside.

It would be hollow if self-modification self-approval were permitted. INV06 is the structural rule that prevents the profile from becoming a more elaborate way for an agent to mark its own self-modifying homework. Adopters who relax it for the self-modification case have decided their profile is something else.

It would be hollow if producer-side responsibility were inverted. A consumer-side verification model dilutes responsibility across an unbounded set of readers, none of whom carry the burden of emitting again when upstream changes. The format only works because producers carry the work and consumers carry only the verification of the last artefact.

The pattern in this list is the same. Every load-bearing rule is brittleness somewhere, a place the format refuses to be flexible, and refuses on purpose. The cost of the spec being right is that it is uncomfortable to use casually. That is the trade.

## Closing: three uncomfortable fields

The earliest essay in this directory ended with the sentence "the system works," and described a small bash script that fired correctly on its first encounter with a real divergence in a repository being edited by multiple agents at once. That essay was about a check; this one is about the spec the check serves. They are about the same thing at different scales.

If a reader walks away from a clean-room reading of DAG-TOML and remembers three fields, the right three are these.

`closure_root`. A SHA-256-or-stronger digest, sitting at the root level of every spec-reserved-kind document, bound to every upstream artefact hash and revocation snapshot the document cites. Change anything upstream, and the digest recomputes to a different value, which invalidates any signed envelope wrapping the document, which the producer is supposed to refresh by signing again. A single field that flips a binary visibility property on every supply-chain change. It is the field that does most of the work the closure-root rule talks about.

`[kind.abstraction_class]` and `[kind.capability_envelope]` on a kind descriptor. A versioned class identifier plus a closed-set capability declaration, both part of the descriptor's canonical bytes and therefore part of its closure root. The descriptor that authorises a class of work declares what that class is and what it is allowed to touch; widening either flips the descriptor's closure root and cascade-breaks every downstream instance that cited it. The spec does the declaration and the propagation. A runtime, outside the spec, does the work of refusing artefacts whose observed behaviour disagrees with the envelope.

`gate-decision.deciding_provider_id` and `gate-decision.deciding_model_family_id`. The two fields that, when `decision.subject_class = "self-modification"`, must both differ from the proposing agent's provider and model family. INV06. The structural rule that an agent cannot close its own self-modification ticket, scoped exactly to the case where it matters, free elsewhere.

Three uncomfortable fields, each pinned in a place the format refuses to be flexible. Nothing else in the spec is more important than these. Everything else is in service of giving them a coherent surface to sit on. A reader who internalises only those three and the discipline of honouring them in the inconvenient cases has internalised most of what the spec exists to teach.

That is the spec, read cold. Used in the spirit it was designed for, the discomfort is the feature. Used against that spirit, the discomfort gets filed off, and the format becomes a different thing that does a different job. Which of those happens is not a spec decision. It is the decision the people building on top of it make, repeatedly, on the days when the rule is in the way.
